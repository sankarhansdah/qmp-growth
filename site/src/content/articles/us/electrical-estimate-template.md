A good electrical estimate names the scope, separates materials from labor, states what is not included, shows the tax line, and gives a price validity date and a deposit. Below: each part, then a full example with the arithmetic. More guides for US contractors are in the [US guides hub](/us/).

## What an electrical estimate should include

Put these on every estimate. The template has a row for each.

- **Your business:** name, phone, email and license number.
- **Client and job address:** the address where the work happens, which may differ from the billing address.
- **Estimate number, date and valid-until date:** material prices move, so say how long the price holds. Thirty days is common practice, not a rule.
- **Scope of work:** what you will do, in plain words ("replace 100A panel with 200A panel, add two 20A circuits").
- **Exclusions:** what you will not do (drywall repair, painting, trenching, utility company fees). This is the section that prevents most arguments.
- **Materials lines:** quantity, unit cost, line total.
- **Labor lines:** hours and hourly rate.
- **Allowances:** items you can't price yet, such as fixtures the client hasn't chosen.
- **Permit and inspection:** who arranges it and who pays.
- **Tax line:** see the tax section below.
- **Deposit and balance:** the amount due to start and the amount due at completion.
- **Change-order wording:** one sentence saying scope changes are priced and agreed in writing before the work starts.
- **Warranty and signature:** a line for the client to accept.

## Filled-in example: panel upgrade

All figures here are illustrative. Use your own supplier prices and rates.

Job: replace a panel with a 200A panel and add two 20A circuits.

| Item | Qty | Unit cost | Line total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200A main breaker panel | 1 | $420.00 | $420.00 |
| 20A single-pole breaker | 2 | $14.00 | $28.00 |
| 12/2 cable (feet) | 100 | $0.80 | $80.00 |
| Connectors, straps, labels (allowance) | 1 | $45.00 | $45.00 |
| **Materials subtotal** | | | **$573.00** |

Now the tax line. The rate and what it applies to depend on your state and the type of job. Two hypothetical cases at an 8% rate:

- **Materials and labor both taxable:** $1,537.60 × 0.08 = $123.008, rounded to $123.01. Total: $1,537.60 + $123.01 = **$1,660.61**.
- **Only materials taxable:** $687.60 × 0.08 = $55.008, rounded to $55.01. Total: $1,537.60 + $55.01 = **$1,592.61**.

Deposit of 25% on the first case: $1,660.61 × 0.25 = $415.15 (rounded). Balance due on completion: $1,660.61 − $415.15 = **$1,245.46**.

The template's tax row applies one rate to the whole subtotal. If only materials are taxable where you work, type the tax amount on that row yourself.

## Sales tax on an electrical estimate: it depends on the job and the state

There is no single national rule. Each state decides whether labor is taxable, whether materials are taxable, and how the answer changes with the kind of contract. Two examples show why you have to check.

### Materials, labor and markup

**New York:** the state tax department says all charges for materials and labor billed to the customer for a repair, maintenance or installation project are taxable, and that includes expenses and markups ([NY Tax Dept.](https://www.tax.ny.gov/pubs_and_bulls/tg_bulletins/st/repair_maintenance.htm)). The same bulletin says capital improvement work is not taxable, and that some projects need a case-by-case review, so whether a given panel job counts as a repair, installation or capital improvement matters. Where the work is taxable, the first scenario above is the right shape: tax on the full subtotal, markup included.

### Lump-sum or itemized pricing

**Texas:** the Comptroller describes two contract types. Under a lump-sum contract, you pay tax on supplies, materials and equipment when you buy them. Under a separated contract, you give suppliers resale certificates instead of paying tax on the materials you incorporate ([Texas Comptroller, Pub. 94-116](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/publications/94-116.php)). So a Texas contractor on a lump-sum job pays tax up front and builds it into the price, while on a separated job the tax shows up as a line on the customer's estimate. That Comptroller publication is dated March 2007, so check the Comptroller's site for current guidance before relying on it.

### How to check your state

1. Search "[your state] department of revenue contractors sales tax".
2. Read the contractor or "repair, maintenance and installation" guidance on the .gov site.
3. Find out whether your job is new construction, a repair, or a capital improvement, since states treat these differently.
4. Ask a tax professional if the guidance is unclear. Local rates (city, county) are added on top in many states.

## Exclusions, allowances and change orders

Write exclusions as specifically as the scope. "Patching and painting by others" is clearer than "finish work not included."

An allowance is a placeholder price for something not yet chosen. State it as such ("light fixtures: $300 allowance; actual cost may differ") so a more expensive fixture adjusts the total instead of becoming a dispute.

For change orders, one sentence on the estimate is enough: changes to the scope are priced and agreed in writing before the work starts.

## Estimate, quote and bid

In everyday use, an **estimate** is your expected price, a **quote** is a firm price for defined work, and a **bid** is a price offered in competition for a job. People use the words loosely and whether a document is legally binding depends on your state and the wording, so don't assume the title decides it. Say what you mean on the page: "This price is valid until [date] for the scope described."

## Before you send: checklist
